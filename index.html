Chapter 011947–1964

The Foundation & Nation-Building Era

How do you turn independence into a republic?

Freedom arrived with Partition, violence and displacement. The new state had to integrate territory, protect citizenship and turn a constitutional promise into working institutions. Under Jawaharlal Nehru, elected government, economic planning and scientific investment became central to the national project. Yet these years also brought restrictions on rights, disputes over state autonomy and war.

Keep in view

The question was never only whether India could build institutions. It was how those institutions would use power: to protect individual rights, pursue social reform, accommodate regional demands and respond to insecurity.

Read the era overview

Begin with the distinction between independence and the republic. The transfer of power did not itself settle the territorial map, end communal violence or put a new Constitution into operation. These processes overlapped, and people experienced them differently.

Then follow the choices about government and development. Elections established a source of political authority, while Parliament, the courts and state governments contested how that authority should be used. The First and Second Plans show a changing set of economic priorities rather than one unvarying programme.

Finally, read diplomacy and scientific ambition alongside their limits. Institutions created long-term capacity, but declarations and plans were not the same as results. Kerala’s dismissal and the China war belong in the same history as the Constitution, technical education and the first rocket launch.

1947DemocracyIndependence, and the trauma of Partition

British rule ended on 15 August 1947. The Independence Act created the independent Dominions of India and Pakistan. Partition brought mass displacement and communal violence alongside the transfer of power.

Context & significance

Independence and becoming a republic were different constitutional milestones. India began as a Dominion and became a republic in January 1950. For displaced families, the immediate concerns were safety, shelter, property and rebuilding livelihoods. Protecting minorities and rehabilitating refugees became urgent responsibilities of the new state.

1949–1950DemocracyA Constitution, then a republic

The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949. Most provisions came into force on 26 January 1950, when India became a republic. The Assembly’s drafting work had begun before independence, with B. R. Ambedkar chairing the Drafting Committee formed in August 1947.

Context & significance

The Constitution combined parliamentary government, fundamental rights, a federal distribution of powers and development goals in the Directive Principles. It was the product of an Assembly and its committees, not a document written by one person. Its original Preamble described a Sovereign Democratic Republic. The new framework soon faced disputes over the reach of rights and the powers needed for social reform.

India held its first general election in 1951–1952. Congress won 364 of the 489 Lok Sabha seats with about 45% of the vote. Nehru continued as prime minister.

The roughly 74% seat share was much larger than the vote share, reflecting the first-past-the-post system. Congress’s dominance existed within a competitive election, not a legal one-party system. Chapter 021964–1975

Wars, Food Security & Social Transition

What does security mean when food is scarce?

After Nehru’s death, leadership passed first to Shastri and then to Indira Gandhi. War, drought and food shortages tested the state. New agricultural methods, dairy cooperatives and public banking changed development policy, while Congress lost ground, split and rebuilt its mandate. By 1975, economic pressure, protests and a contested election had brought government and opposition into open confrontation.

Keep in view

Security had several meanings in these years: defending borders, securing food, finding work and protecting political rights. Measures intended to strengthen the state also raised questions about who controlled its institutions and who benefited from its policies.

Read the era overview

Start with the succession after Nehru. The two leadership changes were constitutional transitions, but they occurred amid military and economic pressure. The war and the Tashkent agreement belong alongside the food crisis, not in a separate story with no domestic consequences.

Next, distinguish the development programmes. Grain production, dairy cooperation and bank ownership involved different institutions. Their political importance was considerable, but announcement dates do not tell the whole story of implementation, regional differences or household welfare.

Finally, follow the political sequence: setbacks in the states, the Congress split, a renewed national mandate, economic strain and mass protests. The court orders of June 1975 close this chapter. The Emergency begins the next one, where its declaration and consequences can be examined together.

Procurement and storage institutions mattered alongside research. Higher national grain output also did not automatically mean that every household had adequate nutrition.
