Chapter 011947–1964

The Foundation & Nation-Building Era

How do you turn independence into a republic?

Freedom arrived with Partition, violence and displacement. The new state had to integrate territory, protect citizenship and turn a constitutional promise into working institutions. Under Jawaharlal Nehru, elected government, economic planning and scientific investment became central to the national project. Yet these years also brought restrictions on rights, disputes over state autonomy and war.

Keep in view

The question was never only whether India could build institutions. It was how those institutions would use power: to protect individual rights, pursue social reform, accommodate regional demands and respond to insecurity.

Read the era overview

Begin with independence and the republic as separate milestones. Partition left urgent questions about territory, citizenship and rebuilding lives. Accession, the Constitution and the 1955 Citizenship Act addressed different parts of the new state. The resulting legal framework also faced early tests over speech, social reform and federal power.

Follow development through institutions, projects and daily life. The First and Second Plans placed different weights on agriculture and heavy industry. Factory protections addressed working conditions; community development and Bhoodan took different approaches to rural change. SBI, LIC, the UGC and the IITs served distinct purposes. Steel plants and Bhakra show construction becoming operational, while decimal coinage and experimental television changed other parts of everyday infrastructure.

Read social reform and regional accommodation alongside national institutions. Civil marriage, Hindu-law reform and laws against untouchability and dowry addressed different inequalities. Family planning entered public-health policy, while Ambedkar’s Buddhist conversion placed dignity and religious choice within the wider social story. Panchayati Raj, state reorganisation, official languages and Nagaland show several ways power and identity were negotiated. Relations with Pakistan and China followed contrasting paths through treaty and war.

1947DemocracyIndependence, and the trauma of Partition

British rule ended on 15 August 1947. The Independence Act created the independent Dominions of India and Pakistan. Partition brought mass displacement and communal violence alongside the transfer of power.

Context & significance

Independence and becoming a republic were different constitutional milestones. India began as a Dominion and became a republic in January 1950. For displaced families, the immediate concerns were safety, shelter, property and rebuilding livelihoods. Protecting minorities and rehabilitating refugees became urgent responsibilities of the new state.

1949–1950DemocracyA Constitution, then a republic

The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949. Most provisions came into force on 26 January 1950, when India became a republic. The Assembly’s drafting work had begun before independence, with B. R. Ambedkar chairing the Drafting Committee formed in August 1947.

Context & significance

The Constitution combined parliamentary government, fundamental rights, a federal distribution of powers and development goals in the Directive Principles. It was the product of an Assembly and its committees, not a document written by one person. Its original Preamble described a Sovereign Democratic Republic. The new framework soon faced disputes over the reach of rights and the powers needed for social reform.

1951–1952DemocracyThe first general election

India held its first general election in 1951–1952. Congress won 364 of the 489 Lok Sabha seats with about 45% of the vote. Nehru continued as prime minister.

Context & significance

The roughly 74% seat share was much larger than the vote share, reflecting the first-past-the-post system. Congress’s dominance existed within a competitive election, not a legal one-party system. Voters initially qualified from age 21; organising rolls and voting across a largely non-literate electorate was a major administrative task.

Chapter 021964–1975

Wars, Food Security & Social Transition

What does security mean when food is scarce?

After Nehru’s death, leadership passed first to Shastri and then to Indira Gandhi. War, drought and food shortages tested the state. New agricultural methods, dairy cooperatives and public banking changed development policy, while Congress lost ground, split and rebuilt its mandate. By 1975, economic pressure, protests and a contested election had brought government and opposition into open confrontation.

Keep in view

Security had several meanings in these years: defending borders, securing food, finding work and protecting political rights. Measures intended to strengthen the state also raised questions about who controlled its institutions and who benefited from its policies.

Read the era overview

Start with the succession after Nehru. The two leadership changes were constitutional transitions, but they occurred amid military and economic pressure. The war and the Tashkent agreement belong alongside the food crisis, not in a separate story with no domestic consequences.

Follow the institutions behind development. Agricultural price advice, grain procurement, dairy cooperatives, bank ownership and general insurance served different purposes. Coal nationalisation and patent reform addressed different parts of industrial policy. Contract-labour and gratuity laws added employment protections. The dates of decisions matter, but each also needs examination through coverage and implementation.

Read political competition, regional change and social action together. New states, language safeguards and opposition victories changed federal politics. Wildlife protection, Chipko and water-pollution regulation took different approaches to environmental concerns, while hockey supplied a major sporting milestone. The Congress split, its renewed mandate, economic strain and protest lead to the court orders of June 1975. The Emergency begins the next chapter.

Chapter 031975–1991

Political Turmoil & the Coalition Turn

How resilient are democratic institutions?

The Emergency restricted political freedom. Voters removed the government in 1977, but the alternative soon fractured. The following decade combined new technology and education programmes with regional conflict, targeted violence and a struggle over representation. By 1991, national power depended increasingly on alliances and outside support.

Keep in view

Elections restored competition after the Emergency. They did not settle questions about executive power, minority security or social representation. The contrast between negotiated peace in Mizoram and escalating violence elsewhere shows why each regional conflict needs its own history.

Read the era overview

Begin with the Emergency and the response to it. Restrictions on political freedom coexisted with legislation on bonded labour and equal remuneration. These laws need examination on their own terms, without treating them as a measure of democratic freedom. The 1977 election, the inquiry into abuses and constitutional safeguards were followed by Janata’s collapse and Congress’s return.

The 1980s combined expanded public responsibilities with major failures of protection. Child-development services, adult literacy, air and forest regulation, and consumer remedies addressed different needs. Satellite services and Rakesh Sharma’s flight marked distinct space achievements; Delhi hosted the Asian Games before the cricket World Cup victory. Punjab, the anti-Sikh killings and Bhopal exposed different forms of violence and vulnerability. Assam and Mizoram pursued distinct settlements, and statehood changed the federal map.

The closing years connect the Bofors allegations and accountability dispute with the 1989 election, followed by Mandal, the Ayodhya mobilisation and the displacement of Kashmiri Pandits. Chapter 041991–2004

Economic Liberalisation & Globalisation

What changes when an economy opens up?

A foreign-exchange crisis brought urgent policy changes in 1991. Trade, investment and industrial rules changed over the following years, while telecom and software services found larger markets. Governments depended increasingly on alliances. Education programmes, local democracy and demands for representation developed alongside communal violence and national security crises.

Keep in view

Opening markets required rules, regulators and public investment. Faster growth in some industries did not mean equal opportunity across regions or occupations. Economic reform also unfolded alongside arguments over social representation, religious identity and the powers of the Union and states.

Read the era overview

Start with the 1991 crisis and the institutions that followed. Rupee adjustment preceded the July Budget and industrial-policy changes. Exchange-rate reform continued through 1992–1994. The securities scandal exposed weaknesses in oversight; electronic equity trading and, later, RTGS changed market and payment infrastructure. PSLV’s successful 1994 flight adds another path of technological development.

Follow public protection alongside changes of government. Coalition bargaining gave regional parties a larger national role. Local-government amendments, PESA and the Bommai judgment addressed different parts of federalism. Human-rights oversight, disability law and the Vishaka guidelines widened routes to protection and participation. National AIDS control and Pulse Polio addressed different public-health challenges.

Internet access, telecom regulation and software exports changed several industries. School nutrition, education, rural roads, Delhi Metro and electricity reform addressed other needs. The Gujarat earthquake exposed the demands of rescue and reconstruction. These developments shared a period with Lahore diplomacy, Kargil and the Parliament attack. Contributory pensions and RTGS began before the 2004 change of government; the next chapter follows the expansion of rights-based welfare.

Chapter 052004–2014

Economic Expansion & Rights-Based Governance

How does growth become an entitlement?

The UPA governments led by Manmohan Singh combined economic expansion with new rights to information, work, education and food. Public-health programmes and space missions reached major milestones. The global financial crisis, terrorist attacks and disputes over corruption tested the government’s capacity and public trust.

Keep in view

A legal entitlement gave people a stronger basis to make a claim. Access still depended on schools, clinics, local administration, timely payments and credible remedies. Growth and new programmes expanded possibilities while inflation, financial volatility and unequal access remained pressing concerns.

Read the era overview

The 2004 change of government was followed by several different forms of public action. RTI opened access to official records, the employment guarantee created a statutory claim to public work, and the rural health mission sought stronger services. Health insurance later addressed hospital costs for eligible poor households. Women’s protections and forest rights dealt with other forms of exclusion.

The financial crisis interrupted the growth story. Monetary easing and fiscal support addressed that shock, while farm debt relief addressed agricultural credit. During the second UPA term, Aadhaar, elementary-school rights, secondary education and a rural livelihoods mission took different approaches to access and opportunity. Environmental adjudication and child-protection law added institutional duties of their own.

The closing years brought anti-corruption protests, the spectrum judgment and demands for women’s safety after the Delhi assault. Food security, land acquisition, sanitation work and company law received new statutory frameworks. The Lokpal law commenced in January 2014, while financial pressure and disputes over implementation continued. Telangana’s formation and the Mars mission crossed into the next era.

What does transformation look like in daily life?

The 2014 election brought a single-party majority within the NDA government. Banking access, sanitation, digital services and tax reform became major priorities. Constitutional disputes, border crises and the pandemic tested institutions. Space missions and diplomacy brought prominent milestones, while the 2024 result increased the importance of coalition partners.

Keep in view

These years remain unfinished history. A launch, a law, an election result and a measured public outcome describe different things. The chapter tracks selected milestones through April 2026, with dates and source records kept separate from claims about lasting success.

Begin with the 2014 political change and the programmes that followed. Banking, sanitation, LPG access and rural tap water addressed different household needs.
